Why it happens

A model learns by minimizing its total mistakes. When 91% of examples say No, the cheapest strategy is to lean hard toward No. Most sick people get scores below the 0.5 cut-off, so they're never flagged.

05 · Making it even

If the data is lopsided, level it.

Rule one: only touch the training data

Every trick below changes what the model learns from. The test set keeps its real 91/9 split, because that's the world the model will face. Balancing the test set too would make the scores look better than reality.

Undersampling: throw away No's

Randomly drop "No" people until both groups are the same size. Simple and fast, but it discards about 90% of the data: No's shrink to .

SMOTE: invent realistic Yes's

SMOTE picks a real heart-disease patient, finds a similar one nearby, and creates a synthetic person somewhere on the line between them. Repeat until the groups are even. The hollow dots are synthetic.

SMOTE on real training rows

Each synthetic point is born on the line between two real neighbors

SMOTEENN: then clean the border

Edited Nearest Neighbours (ENN) removes points whose neighbors mostly disagree with them, clearing the noisy overlap between the groups. SMOTE + ENN is what my notebook used.

Faded points are the ones ENN removes

Class weights make each Yes count about × more in training. Threshold tuning keeps the model as-is but lowers the cut-off for flagging someone, chosen with cross-validation on training data only.

06 · Keeping score

Accuracy was the wrong scoreboard.

Four possible outcomes

Every prediction is one of four things. Here they are for logistic regression trained on SMOTEENN data: true positives (sick, flagged), false negatives (sick, missed), false positives (healthy, flagged) and true negatives (healthy, cleared).

Accuracy

(TP + TN) ÷ everyone

Dominated by the huge true-negative block. With 91% No's, accuracy mostly measures how well you clear healthy people. Fine when classes are balanced and mistakes cost the same; misleading here.

Precision

TP ÷ (TP + FP)

"When the model flags someone, how often is it right?" Low precision means many false alarms, which cost follow-up tests and worry.

Recall

TP ÷ (TP + FN)

"Of the people who are sick, how many did we catch?" For screening this matters most: a missed case goes home untreated. My first model scored .

F1 score

2 · precision · recall ÷ (precision + recall)

The harmonic mean punishes a model that games one side. It ignores true negatives entirely, so the 91% majority can't inflate it. The always-No model scores 0.

ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and Cohen's κ

ROC-AUC asks how well the model ranks sick above healthy at every cut-off, but it looks rosy under imbalance. PR-AUC is stricter: its do-nothing baseline is the positive rate. Cohen's κ measures agreement beyond chance; always-No gets exactly 0.

Try it: move the cut-off

Lower the threshold and you catch more sick people but raise more false alarms. Cross-validation picked for the best F1.

07 · Inside the models

Five models, five ways of thinking.

Every model turns a person's 22 answers into a verdict, but each one gets there differently. Here's what each actually does, on real people from the data, using the real trained models.

Model 1 of 5

Logistic regression weighs the evidence

A real test-set patient, scored by my real model. Switch patients to compare.

Start from an average person

The model's starting point is the score of a perfectly average respondent: a log-odds of about −3.2, or a 4% chance of heart disease.

Each answer nudges the score

Each answer differs from average by some amount, and the model multiplies that by a learned weight. Poor health, age and difficulty walking push right (riskier); being younger or healthier than average pushes left.

z = b + w1x1 + w2x2 + … + w22x22

Squash it into a probability

The total z can be any number. The sigmoid bends it into a probability between 0 and 1. That S-shape is where "logistic" comes from.

p = σ(z) = 1 ⁄ (1 + e−z)

The cut-off decides

At the default cut-off of 0.5, this patient, who really had heart disease, is missed. The tuned cut-off of flags them.

Everyone at once

1,200 test-set people dropped onto the curve, 91% of them healthy as in real life. Most sick people land below 0.5, which is why my first model caught only of every 100.

KNN has no formula to learn. It keeps every training answer, and to judge someone new it lets the k most similar people vote.

The 5 closest people vote

A patient with heart disease appears. KNN measures their distance to everyone, wraps the 5 nearest in a sphere, and goes with the majority: at risk.

Model 4 of 5

AdaBoost learns from its mistakes

A team of "stumps", each a single yes/no cut, built one at a time. Every new stump is trained to fix what the team so far gets wrong.

round 0error ε —say α —

Everyone starts equal

100 people with heart disease and 100 without, from the training sample, on a 2D summary of their answers. Every dot has the same weight, so the same size.

Stump #1: one cut

The best single straight cut puts most red on one side. It still gets plenty wrong; those people are ringed in amber.

Mistakes get heavier

Every misclassified person's weight goes up and every correct one's goes down. The worse the stump, the smaller its say α.

α = ½ ln((1 − ε) ⁄ ε)    wi ← wi · e±α

The next stump chases the heavy ones

Each new cut is pulled toward the people the team keeps getting wrong. Old cuts fade into the background, but they still count.

The team votes

Add every stump's vote, weighted by its say, and many straight cuts stack into a patchwork border. My notebook stacked 750 stumps: accurate, but without balancing it caught only of every 100.

F(x) = sign( Σt αt ht(x) )

Model 5 of 5

A neural network passes signals through layers

My actual trained network: 22 answers, squeezed to 18 numbers by PCA, then 100 hidden neurons and one output. A real patient's answers travel through it as pulses of light.

neurons firing —output —

22 answers go in

Each input node is one encoded answer. Brighter means further from average for this patient.

Compress to 18 numbers

My pipeline standardizes the answers and keeps the 18 principal components that hold 90% of the variation. Every component mixes all 22 answers.

100 hidden neurons

Each neuron takes a weighted sum of the 18 inputs plus a bias. ReLU lets positive totals through and silences the rest, so only some neurons fire for any given person.

hj = max(0, bj + Σi wij xi)

One output

The output neuron is a logistic regression on the 100 hidden neurons: weigh, add, squash into a probability.

p = σ(c + Σj vj hj)

Learning runs backwards

Training compares the output with the truth and sends the error back through every connection (backpropagation), nudging all 2,001 learned weights. Mine did this after every batch of 200 people, for 31 passes over the training set. Unbalanced it caught of every 100; with SMOTEENN, .

09 · Takeaways

Look at the data before trusting the score.

Draft: rewrite in your own voice

A 91% accurate model caught 11% of sick people. Checking the class balance, choosing metrics that match the real cost of mistakes, and rebalancing only the training data turned it into a model that catches about 8 in 10.

Next steps: gradient boosting, probability calibration, cross-validated model selection, thresholds based on the real cost of a missed case, and the newer 2022 survey.
